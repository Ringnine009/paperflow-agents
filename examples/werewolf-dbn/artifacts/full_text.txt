Dynamic Belief Networks and Deep-Thinking Probes for
Multi-agent Social Reasoning
Zhenxiao Guo
College of Electronic and Information Engineering, Tongji University,Shanghai, China
2353905@tongji.edu.cn
Abstract. There are two longstanding problems in social deduction when using multi-agent
systems powered by large language models (LLMs): recursive agreement between
homogeneous agents and stable detection of deception. A framework that uses a combined
Dynamic Belief Network (DBN) and a Deep-Thinking Token Ratio (DTR) probe is
proposed in this paper, which was shown to work with nine-player Werewolf and 3000
simulated games. DBN keeps per-player suspicion estimates across rounds by using
exponential moving average (EMA) smoothing, cutting the positive-feedback loop between
homogeneous models. DTR uses logit-lens to measure vocabulary-distribution changes
along each transformer-layer of an utterance, as a proxy of cognitive load by relying on
cross-architecture inferences. In six controlled conditions, unassisted baseline was a 44.2%
win rate by the villagers. Combined use of DBN and prompted with reasoning increased it to
68.8%, propelled vote accuracy up to 66.6% (from 35.5%), and expedited convergence of
the Brier Score. The strongest identification but the shortest survival was obtained with the
full-module configuration which indicated a non-monotonic dependence of individual
capability on collective payoff that is similar to the handicap principle in evolutionary game
theory.
Keywords: large language model, multi-agent system, social deduction, dynamic belief
network, deep-thinking probe
1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are now important parts of multi-agent systems (MAS) [1].
Contrary to the past designs that used predetermined communication protocols, more MAS today
communicate using natural language and this allows agents to share information, articulate stances,
and negotiate using an open-ended dialogue. Multi-agent studies have therefore taken a new
direction to social reasoning and deliberative communication in complex incomplete-information
settings.
Such capabilities are deemed to be appropriate in social deduction games (SDGs). Werewolf is a
game of partial information, and antagonistic goals: the participants have to determine how credible
the other players are, and preserve their own credibility. The combination of search and planning is
not the best way to reach the optimal solutions, as the most important challenge is to make

inferences about the intentions and strategies of others. SDGs are thus experimented with as
simplified experimental contexts of Theory of Mind and belief reasoning in artificial agents [2, 3].
Sarkar et al. [2] demonstrated that the information disclosure and strategic concealment can be
learned by the LLMs trained on social deduction dialogues. The WOLF benchmark suggested by
Agarwal et al. [4] captures the transcripts of dialogues, votes and identities of the simulated games
of Werewolf allowing the systematic multi-round analysis of belief. One such system, suggested by
Ye et al. [5], is Multi-agent KTO (MaKTO) which employs reinforcement learning on the game
trajectories and greatly increases the win rate. An increased win rate, though, does not necessarily
correspond to more credible social thinking and the dynamics of underlying beliefs are
understudied.
The current systems usually assume that dialogue history is a static context, and they use the
recent utterances and undermine earlier information. This easily results in recursive consensus [6]:
LLMs that see such similar corpora agree on ambiguous facts, minority cues are ignored, and
groupthink degenerates into an echo chamber where false premises are reinforced. In the meantime,
despite biased reasoning, LLMs are able to produce structurally rigorous text, and it is hard to
determine the truthfulness of a text based on semantic analysis.
Kostka and Chudziak [7] asserted that in MAS, Theory of Mind has to be assessed with the
explicit representation of internal belief states of agents. Chen et al. [8] introduced the DTR
measure, estimating the effort of reasoning based on prediction change between the layers of a
neural network. Their experiments showed familiar patterns of computation when models are
confronted with complicated reasoning or uphold strategic narratives.
Expanding on the foregoing, this paper suggests a framework to integrate probabilistic belief
tracking and probes of cross-model reasoning. To break the positive-feedback loop, DBN keeps per-
player suspicion through EMA smoothing. A cross-model DTR probe, an analysis of change in
layerwise reasoning with a different-architecture model, will supply auxiliary deception cues
separately, without the aid of a cross-model probe. Each module is tested on 6 controlled groups that
measure the independent contribution and interaction effects of the modules, and indicate a non-
monotonic relationship between individual identification ability and collective payoff in the game.
2. Methods
2.1. Experimental setup and baselines
The experiment is a nine-player Werewolf game that runs on top of AgentScope, with DeepSeek-
V3.2 serving as the backend of all 9 agents, a 512-token maximum output, and a soft limit on
utterances is 500 Chinese characters. To ensure metric consistency, the target agent is pegged to the
role of ordinary villager in each of the games, and to remove positional bias, its seat is rotated across
the games.
There are six module configurations (Table 1). Using MaKTO-Proxy to approximate the
reinforcement-learning approach of Ye et al. [5] at inference time: Winning dialogues on the
villager-side at WOLF are presented as a few-shot chain-of-thought exemplar [9] and fed into the
system prompt, providing strategic improvement without extra training. Local Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct
(N = 36 layers) is used in the DTR detector. Each setup plays 500 games, amounting to 3000.

2.4. DTR probe
DTR measures vocabulary-distribution evolution across layers of LLM as a proxy of cognitive load
using the logit-lens method [8]. It follows a cross-model scheme: the utterance of the target agent is
inputted into an independently deployed probe model (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct), which processes its
own layerwise hidden states, without having access to the parameters of the target model.

3. Results and analysis
3.1. Overall social reasoning performance
The summary of the core metrics is given in Table 2. The information disadvantage of the villager
side resulted in the vanilla baseline (Group A) winning at a rate of 44.2% that is lower than random.
Immediate reasoning improvement and historical belief anchoring increased gains were made
independently by MaKTO-Proxy (Group B, 54.6%), and standalone DBN (Group C, 53.8%). Group
E (MaKTO + DBN, 68.8%) had an increase in win-rate of approximately 24.6 percentage points
(hereafter pp) which is similar to the combination of B and C's independent gains (~20 pp) and is
thus indicative of the two modules acting in orthogonal directions and having additive effects as
opposed to redundant. The percent accuracy of the vote rose monotonically (35.5% A, 66.6% F)
with no plateaus. Group F was the poorest in terms of mean survival (2.33 rounds) but a win rate
was slightly lower than E (68.2% vs. 68.8%). Section 4 revert to this seemingly contradictory
situation.

4. Discussion
Group F has the highest accuracy of its votes (66.6%) but a win rate a bit lower than E (66.6% vs.
68.2%). The mechanism is shown by the difference in mean survival (2.33 vs. 2.73 rounds): the
combination of the full-module results in the identification efficiency so high that the target agent
shows an apparent information advantage in the course of discussion that forces the opponent to kill
it early. Once removed, other voting contributions are reduced to zero, which limits the macro win
rate. In incomplete-information games, the more information analyst has, the more the game reveals
it, and so the more it is the number one to be eliminated.
This structurally corresponds to the signaling theory and the handicap principle. According to the
signaling theory espoused by Spence [12], signals are always associated with costs as well as the
transfer of information. Accurate voting in the Werewolf game is a capability signal voters interpret
as a threat signal. Zahavi handicap principle [13] has been used to capture a similar parallel in
evolutionary game theory where costly displays of honesty are a manifestation of quality but capture
the attention of predators, thus creating a conflict between ability and survival. Group F translates
this paradoxical scenario into a multi-agent game. The implication of the finding is that individual
capability gains are not necessarily associated with equal collective payoff, and marginal returns
may decline (or even be negative). Information-exposure control and reasoning enhancement should
be jointly considered in the future in multi-agent strategy design.
MaKTO-Proxy and DBN have varying dimensions of capability. The reduced BCR of B (1.60
rounds) is indicative of the short-run impact of few-shot chain-of-thought on first-round judgement
and the rapid convergence of the Brier Score is indicative of the longer-run cross-round calibration
benefit of DBN. The combination of the two modules is therefore additive due to the fact that the
two modules are aimed at fast discovery and stable calibration respectively. The 68.8 percent win
rate and 23.8 pp cumulative vote-accuracy lift (near the independent sum of B and C) of E directly
confirms the existence of orthogonal stacking.
Cross-model DTR is still exploratory. Absolute discriminative power is limited by the
architectural difference between DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen2.5-3B. DTR however brought about
visible marginal gains in both situations. It increased vote accuracy and win rate by 3.4 pp and 4.8
pp, respectively, without MaKTO (C→D), which translated directly. As the MaKTO appeared
(E→F), vote accuracy increased by 7.3 pp and win rate decreased by 0.6 pp because survival has
decreased by 2.59 to 2.33 rounds, which is in line with the above ability-survival tension. DTR

5. Conclusions
This paper suggested a hybrid system that consists of a Dynamic Belief Network and cross-model
Deep-Thinking Token Ratio probe, aiming at recursive agreement and deception detection in multi-
agent multi-agent social deduction using LLMs. The strategy was tested in 3000 simulated games
with nine players in Werewolf with six controlled conditions.
The unassisted baseline had a win rate of 44.2% by the villagers. It was lifted by Standalone
DBN to 53.8% by cross-round belief stabilization, and raised it to 68.8% by combining DBN with
MaKTO-Proxy. The two modules are calibrating beliefs and the quality of immediate reasoning
respectively and their combined gains are nearly equal to their sums, thus showing orthogonal
contribution. The cross-model DTR probe added further vote-accuracy improvements in both
conditions (C→D: +3.4 pp, E→F: +7.3 pp), which provided initial support that cross-architecture
cognitive-load cues are able to represent meaningful information about deception.
The complete-module design indicated a non-monotonic dependence between personal
identifying skill and team game compensation: the most proficient agent received the initial
elimination, topping the group compensation rate. This capability-survival conflict is analogous to
the handicap principle of the evolutionary game theory and implies that the design of strategies of
future multi-agent strategies should be designed to address information-exposure control, as well as
the design of reasoning, in a co-dependent manner.
Several limitations remain. The test was limited to one game condition and homogeneous model
backends thus cross-game and cross-model generalization was not tested. Future research directions
are Bayesian-based DBN with dynamic alpha scheduling, multi-round DTR time aggregation,
heterogeneous populations of agents, and generalization to other less-than-complete-information
contexts like Avalon or Diplomacy.
